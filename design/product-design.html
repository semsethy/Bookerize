Library

  1. Everything you're reading, and exactly where you stopped

    The shelf is the home screen. Covers are page one of the book itself, rendered by the PDF engine — so a book looks like itself without you having to supply artwork or metadata.

    Under each cover: a progress bar and the real page number, not a percentage. "p. 47 / 137" tells you something; "34%" doesn't.

    Import

    Books come in from Files or iCloud Drive. A scanned PDF has no text layer, so word lookup and explanations can't work on it — the app checks that at import and says so plainly, rather than letting you find out three chapters in.

  2. Opening a book returns you to the page, never the beginning

    Tap a cover and you land on page 47 with a brief confirmation that fades. Your position is written on every page turn, so a crash, a force-quit, or a flat battery costs you nothing.

    Then the chrome gets out of the way. No toolbar, no page slider, no floating buttons — the page is the whole screen. One tap brings the bar back when you want it.

  3. Pages turn like paper

    Swipe and the page lifts, curls, and drops — with the weight and shadow of a real leaf, not a slide transition.

    Why it's built last

    The curl animates a picture of the page, but selecting text needs the live page. So the app shows the real page at rest and swaps to the curl only during the swipe — you can't select text mid-swipe anyway, so the switch is invisible.

  4. Long-press a word you don't quite know

    A card rises from the bottom edge with the definition — instantly, offline, and free. The dictionary ships inside the app, so this works on a plane, on the underground, and on a bad hotel connection.

    Why the bottom, not a bubble

    A popover over the word covers the sentence you were reading and sits where your thumb isn't. A card at the bottom leaves the text visible above it and lands where your hand already is.

  5. Then ask what it means here

    A dictionary tells you what a word means in general. The whole point of this app is the second question: what is it doing in this sentence. One tap, and the answer writes itself into the card.

    It streams a word at a time on purpose. Three seconds behind a spinner feels broken; the same three seconds with words arriving feels quick.

    Asked once, ever

    Every answer is stored against that word in that sentence. Look it up again next month and it appears instantly, offline, at no cost.

  6. Select a sentence that won't sit still

    Drag across text and you get the standard iOS selection you already know — grab handles, real highlighting — plus one extra item on the toolbar: Explain.

    Highlight and Note are there too, because the sentence you don't understand and the sentence you want to keep are often the same one.

  7. Get it back in plain words

    A sheet slides up with the original sentence at the top and a simpler version beneath it. Not a summary and not a translation — the same meaning, in easier language, so you can go back and read the author's version and finally see it.

    Dismiss it with a swipe. You're back on the page, exactly where you were.

  8. On a picture page, long-press does nothing at all

    A third of this book — 46 pages of 137 — is full-page illustration with no text behind it. Long-press there and nothing happens: no error, no toast, no buzz.

    An app that shouts "no text found" on a third of the book teaches you it's broken. Silence teaches you there's nothing there, which is true.

  9. What you marked, kept in one place

    Highlights and notes collect per book, newest first, each with the page it came from. Tap one to jump back to it on the page.

    Highlights are anchored to the text, not to a rectangle on the screen — so they stay put when you change the reading ground or rotate the phone.

  10. Three grounds, chosen for the hour

    Paper for daylight, sepia for evening, night for the dark. Night isn't an inverted page — the paper is dimmed and the ink lifted, so illustrations don't turn into negatives.

    Optional paper grain sits under the text at very low contrast. It's off by default.

  11. With no signal, it's still a dictionary

    The definition is local, so it appears as usual. The in-context line is replaced by one quiet sentence saying what needs a connection — no red, no alert, no retry loop.

    Anything you've already looked up in this book stays available, because it was saved the first time.

Design language

Two materials, held apart

Everything in the app is either the book or the instrument, and the two never borrow each other's clothes. The book is serif on paper. The instrument — cards, toolbars, labels — is the iOS system face on glass. You always know which one is speaking.

Behaviour

The rules that hold when things go wrong

Most of an app's character shows up in the failure cases. These are decided, not open.

  • Failure is silent where absence is normalNo text under your finger means nothing happens. Errors are reserved for things that are actually wrong.
  • Nothing is ever asked twiceEvery definition and explanation is stored against its exact word and sentence, so a second look costs nothing and works offline.
  • Offline degrades, it doesn't breakThe dictionary is local and always works. Only the in-context lines go quiet, and they say so in one plain sentence.
  • Your position is never lostWritten on every page turn, so force-quitting is a safe way to close the app.
  • Words are found by shape, not spacesChapter headings are often letter-spaced — T H E B O O K. Long-pressing one returns the whole word, not one letter.
  • Your key never leaves the serverThe app talks to a small proxy that holds the API key. Nothing sensitive ships inside the app, on your phone or anyone else's.

Yours to decide

Four calls I'd rather you made

Everything above is settled enough to build. These four aren't, and all of them are cheap to change now and annoying to change later.

01

Is it called Bookerize?

It's a working title. The name is baked into the App Store listing and the icon at TestFlight, so it's worth deciding before then — but trivial to change today.

02

Shelf or list?

Covers look better. A list holds far more books legibly. With a handful of books the shelf wins; if you'll keep fifty, it doesn't.

03

What should the background do?

You asked for a subtle animation. Paper grain that sits still? A gradient that drifts over minutes? Something that reacts to the page turn? They're very different amounts of work.

04

One tap, or two?

Right now the in-context meaning is a second tap. The alternative is fetching it the moment you long-press — faster, but it spends money on every lookup, including the ones the dictionary already answered.
